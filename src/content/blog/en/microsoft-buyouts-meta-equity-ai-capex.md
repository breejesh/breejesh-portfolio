> **TL;DR**
> * **The Catalyst:** On October 26, Microsoft offered voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees in the US, Canada, India and the UK, roughly 27% of its 223,000-person workforce. The same day, Meta filed to sell $35 billion of stock and preferred shares now, with a planned $60 billion at-the-market program to follow in 2027.
> * **The Mechanism:** Meta guides 2026 capex at $135 to $145 billion against $97 billion of operating cash flow in the first three quarters. The gap has to be filled from outside. Microsoft spent $41 billion on capex in a single quarter. Neither company is in trouble, but both are now managing the AI buildout as a financing problem, not just an engineering one.
> * **The Outlook:** The buyouts will not fund the data centers. The equity raise mostly will. The interesting signal is that the richest companies in the world have started paying for AI the way capital-hungry startups do: by trimming people and selling shares.

Two announcements landed within hours of each other on Monday, and they read like a before-and-after picture of the same problem.

Microsoft told roughly a quarter of its staff they could leave with a package. Meta told investors it would sell them up to $95 billion of new stock. Neither company is short of revenue. Microsoft's last quarter brought in $90 billion, up 18%. Meta's third-quarter sales rose 26%.

So why are two of the most profitable companies on earth acting like they need money?

## What Each Company Announced

### Microsoft: the Voluntary Exit Offer

| Item | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Eligible employees | About 60,000, roughly 27% of the workforce |
| Countries | US, Canada, India, UK |
| Eligibility | Level 70 and below, which reaches into senior leadership |
| Deadline | Accept by the first week of December; leave December 31 |
| Severance (US) | Minimum eight weeks' pay, tenure-based; continued stock vesting, healthcare and career services |
| Expected take-up | 3% to 5%, per Fortune, based on similar programs elsewhere |
| Context | First-ever voluntary retirement offer in April; about 4,800 layoffs in July; 15,000+ in 2025 |

Chief People Officer Amy Coleman's memo was unusually candid: "The Voluntary Exit Offer is new for us. We'll learn from it."

### Meta: up to $95 billion of equity

From Meta's free-writing prospectus filed with the SEC on October 26:

| Piece | Size | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mandatory convertible preferred | About $12.5 billion | Depositary shares that convert to common stock around August 2029 |
| Class A common via forward sale | $22.5 billion (plus a $3.4 billion option) | Banks sell now; Meta receives cash when it settles the forwards, any time before November 2027 |
| At-the-market program | Up to $60 billion | Class C common stock, not expected to start until Q1 2027 |
| Cornerstone interest | Up to $5 billion combined | Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom indicated interest in buying Class A shares; non-binding |

Meta created the new Class C share class on October 21, five days before the filing. The forward-sale structure means the $22.5 billion common offering brings in no cash on day one. Meta draws it down when it needs it.

## The Number That Explains Meta

Here is the arithmetic from the same filing:

| Metric | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| 2026 capex guidance | $135 to $145 billion |
| Operating cash flow, first three quarters of 2026 | $97 billion |
| Implied full-year operating cash flow (my extrapolation) | About $130 billion |
| Cash and marketable securities, end of Q3 | About $82 billion |

At the midpoint of guidance, Meta will spend more on capex this year than its entire operating cash flow. That is before dividends, buybacks or anything else. A company in that position has three ways to close the gap: run down cash, borrow, or sell equity. Meta has said it will use "a balanced mix of operating cash flow, investment-grade debt, strategic partnerships, and equity capital," and warned it will need to raise "hundreds of billions of dollars" over several years.

Equity is the expensive option. It permanently dilutes existing shareholders, and investors read it as a sign that management thinks the stock is fully valued. Meta choosing it anyway, with an Aa3/AA- credit rating that would let it borrow cheaply, says something about how much capital it expects to need. Debt markets are deep, but no single issuer can keep coming back for $100 billion a year without paying up.

The cornerstone investors deserve their own sentence. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom are among Meta's largest suppliers. They are offering to buy up to $5 billion of the stock whose proceeds will pay for their chips. It is a smaller version of the [circular financing pattern](/blog/en/nvidia-750b-circular-finance) that has run through this whole cycle: suppliers funding customers so the customers can keep buying.

## The Number That Explains Microsoft (and Why It Is Not the Capex)

It is tempting to read the buyouts as Microsoft cutting people to pay for GPUs. The numbers do not support that.

Take Fortune's expected take-up of 3% to 5% and apply it to the 60,000 eligible. That is roughly 1,800 to 3,000 people. Even at a generous $300,000 fully loaded annual cost per person (my assumption, not a reported figure), the saving is $0.5 to $0.9 billion a year. Microsoft spent $41 billion on capex in the June quarter alone. The buyouts would cover a few days of data center spending.

So the program is about something else. Three readings, in order of how much I believe them:

1. **Reshaping, not shrinking.** A voluntary program lets people self-select out of roles that AI tooling is changing fastest, without the legal and morale cost of targeted layoffs. Microsoft gets to see who leaves before deciding what to cut.
2. **Operating margin optics.** Depreciation from the buildout will flow into operating expenses for years. Every dollar of headcount cost removed now protects the margin line that investors watch when that depreciation arrives.
3. **A signal to investors.** Microsoft reports earnings on Wednesday. Announcing cost discipline two days before is not a coincidence.

The first reading is the one that matters for engineers. When a company opens exits to 27% of its workforce, including senior levels, it is saying the org chart it has is not the one it wants in two years.

## Two Strategies, One Problem

| | Microsoft | Meta |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lever pulled | Operating costs (people) | Capital structure (equity) |
| Size relative to the problem | Small; savings under $1 billion a year at expected take-up | Large; up to $95 billion |
| Reversible? | Mostly; hiring can resume | No; shares issued stay issued |
| What it tells investors | Margins will be defended | Capex will not be cut |
| Who bears the cost | Employees who leave, and teams that absorb their work | Existing shareholders, through dilution |

Neither is a sign of distress. Both are signs that AI infrastructure has become large enough to change how the biggest companies in tech run their finances. Two years ago, Big Tech funded capex from cash flow and still had tens of billions left over for buybacks. That is no longer true for Meta, and the margin of safety is thinner for everyone.

---

## What to Watch

1. **Microsoft's earnings on Wednesday.** The capex number and any comment on take-up targets. If the company guides capex higher again, the buyouts look more like cost offsets than reshaping.
2. **Pricing of Meta's preferred shares.** The dividend rate on the mandatory convertible will show how much investors are charging Meta for the privilege of funding superintelligence.
3. **Whether the chipmakers follow through.** The $5 billion indication is non-binding. A smaller final number would say more than a press release.
4. **Copycats.** Google has offered buyouts this year in the US and UK. If Amazon or Alphabet follow Meta into equity markets, the pattern stops being one company's choice and becomes the sector's funding model.

---

## The Leftover

Amy Coleman's line, "We'll learn from it," is the most honest sentence either company published on Monday.

Nobody running these companies has financed anything this large before. Meta's filing calls the equity "a complementary source of capital." Microsoft calls its buyout "a new choice." Both are polite ways of saying the old playbook, where cash flow covered everything, has run out of pages.
